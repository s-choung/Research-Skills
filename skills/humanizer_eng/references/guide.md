# Reference Guide: Human Writing Principles

Consult when SKILL.md rules leave a judgment call unclear. Compressed from the original 12-level prompt.

---

## Banned Vocabulary (English)

Always remove or replace:

delve, tapestry, nuanced, multifaceted, pivotal, robust, foster, enhance, leverage (verb), navigate (figurative), underscore, serves as, holistic, comprehensive, empower, impactful, innovative, transformative, groundbreaking, revolutionary

Mechanical transitions (usually delete, replace with actual logical relationship):
furthermore, moreover, additionally, it is worth noting, not just X but also Y

Empty intensifiers (delete when used without evidence):
crucial, vital, paramount, essential, critical

AI opening/closing phrases (always delete):
absolutely, certainly, great question, I hope this helps, feel free to ask, moving forward, going forward, at this juncture, in conclusion, key takeaways

## Inflated Phrases -> Plain Replacements

- due to the fact that -> because
- has the ability to -> can
- in order to -> to
- in the event that -> if
- with regard to -> about
- is in the process of -> (use the verb directly)
- make a decision -> decide
- conduct an investigation -> investigate
- provide assistance to -> help
- in spite of the fact that -> although
- for the purpose of -> to

## Sentence Diagnostics

Three tests, in order:

1. **Actor test**: Who does what? Passive voice ("improvements were made") -> active voice.
2. **Event test**: What specifically happened? Category ("faced challenges") -> name the event.
3. **Deletion test**: Does the paragraph survive without this sentence? If yes -> delete.

All three fail -> pure AI filler. Do not invent information to fill the gap. Delete or leave visible.

## Paragraph Repair vs. Replacement

More than half the sentences in a paragraph fail all three tests -> don't fix individual sentences. Replace the paragraph. Write the paragraph's intended claim in one sentence and build from there.

More than half the paragraphs in a document need replacement -> rewrite the whole document. The original becomes reference material.

## Opening Patterns

Common AI opening failure: "In this article, we will explore...", "X is an important topic in today's world."

Test: Could this sentence open any piece on the same general topic? If yes -> AI opening. Replace.

Openings that work:
- Specific scene (date, place, person)
- Contradiction (two facts collide)
- Frame-breaking fact
- Direct claim (a sentence that could be wrong)

For reports/proposals: finding first, then methodology. Not "This report analyzes..." but "Bounce rate increased 40%. The cause isn't pricing — it's onboarding."

## Second Paragraph Rule

Retreating to context/background after a strong opening is AI's most consistent structural pattern. The second paragraph must advance the opening's claim. Context goes third or later.

## Counter-Arguments

AI pattern: "Some argue X. However, Y is also important. Both perspectives have merit."

Correct approach: State the strongest objection in its strongest form -> respond with specific evidence -> name what the evidence can't settle. The conclusion should be weaker than the original claim. That weakness is honesty, not a defect.

## Register Modulation

The same content changes depending on the reader:
- Expert: use terms without definition, skip context
- General audience: explain terms, state uncertainties
- Stakeholder: use "you", translate abstract decisions into personal outcomes

If the reader isn't specified, infer from the text (terms used without definition, consequences assumed without explanation, pronoun patterns).

## Pacing

AI documents give equal depth to every part. Human writing accelerates and decelerates.

- Slow down at the most important points (longer paragraphs, more evidence)
- Clear conclusions arrive quickly
- After saying something important, stop with a short sentence

## Adversarial Self-Check

Final review questions:

1. What is the weakest claim in the document? Is the evidence sufficient?
2. Which example carries the most weight? Does it actually support the claim?
3. Which transition carries the most weight? Is it hiding a logical leap?
4. Which part sounds most confident? What would a hostile reader bring up?
5. Reading only the first and last sentences — do they belong to the same argument?

## Certainty Gradient

- Based on direct observation -> state directly
- Based on inference -> show the reasoning
- Based on incomplete information -> name what's missing
- Under debate -> state separately what's known, what's contested, and why

"I don't know" is one of the most human sentences, and one of the rarest in AI output.
